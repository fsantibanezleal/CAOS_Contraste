# Case C01, retail cards PD: champion vs challenger

- **Category:** credit-scoring
- **Source:** real samples: UCI Default of Credit Card Clients (Taiwan, Yeh 2009, DOI 10.24432/C55S3H) and UCI Statlog
  German Credit (Hofmann 1994, DOI 10.24432/C5NC77), both CC BY 4.0 and mirror-allowed.
- **One line:** does a monotone WoE scorecard match the challengers the literature says can beat it, once
  calibration is required, and what do the validation tests say when the population shifts?

## Why this case exists

The PD scorecard is the model a validator meets most often, and the champion against which every machine-learning
challenger is argued. The literature reports that tree ensembles beat a logistic scorecard by a few AUC points on
retail data (Lessmann et al. 2015) and that deep networks do not beat boosted trees on tabular credit data
(Gunnarsson et al. 2021); C01 measures it on held-out data with the paired test, requires every challenger to be
calibrated, and uses perturbations of known size to show what each validation test detects. Its controls: the
constant PD (AUC exactly 0.5) and the strongest single variable.

## Formalisation

- **Inputs.** Taiwan: the limit, education, six months of repayment status, bill statements and payments (20 inputs);
  German: 17 attributes. Sex, marital status, personal status, foreign-worker status and age are never inputs; sex is
  kept only to report discrimination by group.
- **Codes outside the codebook** (education 0, 5, 6; repayment status -2 and 0) are flagged by contract 1, counted
  (345 and 25,939 Taiwan records) and kept as their own bins.
- **Split.** A locked stratified holdout of 30%; a calibration slice of 20% of the rest; the training slice (16,800
  Taiwan records); repeated stratified five-fold folds inside it. Seed 42.
- **Ladder.** P0 constant and single variable; P1 WoE scorecard (optbinning, statsmodels Logit; 600 points at odds
  50 to 1, PDO 20); P2a L1 logistic on WoE; P2b PLTR; P3 EBM (five pairs, 64 bins); P4 monotone LightGBM with an
  isotonic map, XGBoost as cross-check; P5 TabPFN v2 on the German twin. A side ladder tests reject inference on the German twin's known rejected population (below).
- **Variants.** holdout; covariate drift moderate (weights exp(0.25 z) on the standardised September repayment
  status) and severe (exp(0.6 z)); prior shift x1.5; label noise 5%; small sample (2,000 training rows); German twin.

Read: on 9,000 held-out Taiwan records the monotone GBM ranks better than the scorecard by about 0.018 AUC, and the
paired test says the gain is real; PLTR and the EBM gain as much, the L1 regression does not. On 300 German records no
rung beats the scorecard significantly. Under covariate drift with P(default | x) unchanged the calibrated models stay

rejects, as it must. At 9,000 obligors Hosmer-Lemeshow flags the champion on the plain holdout (p = 0.015, amber)
while its level is right, which is why the case's severity policy weighs the level above the fit.

**Re-baked in release 0.04.000** after case C22 measured two of the tests. Hosmer-Lemeshow on the holdout now has as
many degrees of freedom as groups, because the PDs were fitted on other data; with G - 2, as before, the test rejects
right PDs more than one time in nine at the 5% level on such samples, and the champion's p was 0.005 (red). The test of
the AUC against the initial AUC now adds the variance of the calibration slice's AUC, an extension of the ECB formula
the result states: the ECB treats the initial AUC as known, and with one estimated on a slice of similar size the test
rejects about one time in six at 5% when nothing changed. The champion's p on the holdout moves from 0.092 to 0.226,
and LightGBM's from 0.026 (amber, a finding) to 0.132 (green, no finding). No AUC, PD, PSI or capital figure changed.

**Capital** (release 0.03.000). The Taiwan cards are qualifying revolving retail, every card a revolver: Basel III
defines a transactor by twelve months of repayment history (BCBS d424, standardised paragraph 56 and IRB paragraph
25) and the data hold six. At 80% approved, LGD 50% and Basel III, the scorecard's book needs NT$28.8 million of
capital (8% of the RWA) and the monotone GBM's NT$27.7 million, 4.2% less: the better ranking lets in less risk at the
same approval rate. The gap is 10.3% under severe drift and reverses to +13.5% for the GBM trained on 2,000 rows; on
the 300 German loans (other retail) the GBM's book needs 28.3% less. The whole evaluated book needs about 8% of its
EAD; Basel II asks exactly 6% more (its 1.06 factor; no PD floor binds), and CRR3 the same as Basel III. Counting the
1,570 holdout accounts that repaid in full or did not use the card in all six months as transactors changes nothing:
no PD of the scorecard lies below the revolvers' 0.1% floor.

## Reject inference on a known rejected population

An application scorecard learns from the applicants a legacy policy accepted, so the sample it is fitted on is selected. Reject
inference tries to repair that with the applicants who were turned away and whose outcomes are never seen. It can only be tested
where those outcomes are known, so the German twin's 1,000 applicants, all labelled, stand for the population: a legacy policy is
simulated on 700 of them (it accepts the best 60%), the outcomes of the rejected are hidden, each rung is fitted on what a lender
would have, and the rungs are scored on the other 300, which no policy screened (the accept-all holdout of Kozodoi et al. 2025,
DOI 10.1016/j.ejor.2025.01.040). The 100 repetitions redraw the split, the policy's noise and the parcelling labels; every interval
is the 95% percentile interval of that mean over bootstrap resamples of the repetitions, so it measures the variation of the
method on these 1,000 applicants, not the sampling of a wider population. The learner is the same for every rung (an L2 logistic
regression, C = 1, never tuned), so only the treatment of the rejected differs.

- **Rungs.** The oracle (every outcome known, the bound nobody can fit); accepted only (the bias to repair); augmentation by the inverse
  acceptance probability, floored at 0.05 (Banasik and Crook 2007); a hard cut-off, the riskiest rejected labelled bad in the share the
  model expects; parcelling into ten score bands at the accepted applicants' bad rate, and the same with a prudence factor of 2
  (the practitioner rule that the rejected are worse, 2 to 5 times, Siddiqi 2017); fuzzy augmentation, every rejected entering twice with
  weights p and 1 - p.
- **Policies.** Acceptance depends on the standardised logit of the inputs plus noise plus kappa times the standardised outcome, kappa
  the weight of the underwriter's private signal: 0 (the control: the outcome plays no part once the inputs are known), 0.25, 0.5 and
  1. A first probe at kappa = 1 alone let in 2.8% defaulters against 30% in the population, a book no lender has, so the strengths became
  a ladder before any other policy was run; the middle one, kappa = 0.5, is the policy the hypotheses are judged on. The design and the
  hypotheses below were written before the run and not changed after it.
- **Bias.** The mean PD minus the observed default rate on the accept-all holdout; the reduction is one minus the ratio of the rung's
  absolute mean bias to the accepted-only model's, with a paired bootstrap interval.

| Policy (kappa) | Defaulters among accepted | Accepted-only bias, points | AUC accepted only | AUC oracle |

| inputs only (0) | 18.2% | -1.32 (-1.82 to -0.81) | 0.7613 | 0.7827 |

**Hypotheses, with their verdicts.** H1, under the moderate private signal the accepted-only model under-states the default rate of the full
population (its interval below 0): **holds**, by 17.19 points (the accepted-only PD averages 12.8% against 30.0% observed). H2, at least one
rung cuts the absolute level bias by 25% or more with an interval above 0: **holds, for one rung only**, parcelling with a prudence factor of 2
(44%, 41% to 48%); the four rungs that assume the policy looked only at the inputs (augmentation, the hard cut-off, parcelling at the
accepted rate, fuzzy augmentation) leave the bias where it was, 0% to 2%. H3, no rung ranks the full population better than the accepted-only
model by more than 0.01 AUC: **does not hold**, parcelling with the prudence factor gains 0.0208 (0.0171 to 0.0243); every other rung ranks
worse than accepted only, by 0.0002 to 0.0365.

**Read.** The acceptance criterion of the reject-inference ladder (the bias measured, and each rung's reduction with its interval) is met, and the result is the
sceptical one the literature predicts (Hand and Henley 1993; Crook and Banasik 2004). Reject inference that works from the inputs cannot
see what the underwriter saw, so it cannot repair the level, and it costs ranking; the one rung that helps does so because its
prudence factor encodes a guess about the rejected, and the factor is not free: with no private signal (the control) the same rung
over-corrects to +10.07 points (a reduction of -663%), with the weak signal it lands at +1.24 points, with the moderate one it recovers
44% and with the strong one 6%. Only an accept-all sample, as here, can tell the analyst which of those worlds the book is in. The limits: the
policy is simulated, the private signal is a modelling choice and kappa = 0.5 still gives a book of 8.9% defaulters, the learner is one
regularised logistic regression, and 1,000 applicants are few. The result is in `data/derived/C01/german-twin.json`
(`outputs.reject_inference`), drawn in the Ladder of the German twin.

## Validation anchor

The perturbed variants are the anchor: each has a known effect that a sound test must detect (or must not), and the
expected-range checks hold the bake to it. The live scorers are anchored to the pipeline by the parity test (1e-9).
