\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\paragraph{The problem in its context.} Banks set capital and provisions with internal models, and supervisors keep finding deficiencies
in those models that raise capital or block an approval. The euro-area review of internal models covered 200 on-site investigations at 65
banks and reported more than 5,800 findings, about 30 percent of them of high severity, and the supervisory decisions that followed raised the
risk-weighted assets of the models assessed by about 12 percent \citep{fr:ecbtrim}. The weakness found most often is the evidence behind the
model: the validation analyses and the quantitative thresholds for back-testing, discriminatory power and stability are missing or are not
measured. The pressure has not eased. In 2025 the euro-area supervisor launched 77 investigations of internal models and found about 16
findings in each, almost half of them of high severity \citep{fr:ecbar2025}; management overlays, which cover roughly a quarter of the loan loss
coverage of performing books, have become permanent instead of transitional \citep{fr:ecboverlays}; and the supervisor has said that it will seek
to ensure that application packages do not include models with material deficiencies \citep{fr:ecbnl2025}.

The rules that frame the evidence change on fixed dates in several jurisdictions at once. The euro-area guide to internal models asks institutions for
quantitative thresholds in the back-testing, discriminatory power, override and stability analyses \citep{fr:ecbegim2026}; the British principles of
model risk management took effect on 17 May 2024 for firms with an internal model approval \citep{fr:pra2023}; the Canadian guideline applies from 1 May 2027
to all federally regulated financial institutions in proportion to their size and risk \citep{fr:osfie23}; and the United States letter of 17 April 2026
replaced the guidance of 2011 \citep{credit-scoring:sr262}. In Chile the supervisor proposed in August 2026 that banks may submit internal models for
individual exposures from January 2028, with the provisioning adjustments taking effect in January 2031, and estimated 571 million United States dollars of
additional provisions on individually assessed commercial loans across the banks if the proposed model applied without their own models
\citep{fr:cmf2026}. Capital floors make every model result a capital question: the European Banking Authority projects a 4.7 percent average rise in
minimum Tier 1 capital once the output floor applies \citep{fr:eba2026}. Machine-learning credit scoring enters the same decisions under the high-risk
rules of the European artificial intelligence act \citep{fr:aiact}. No primary source was found for a supervisory fine or capital add-on tied by name to weak
model validation, nor for the size of the model inventories or validation backlogs of banks; the argument above rests on the supervisory
findings and the dates of the rules, not on those figures.

The people who build a model and the people who validate it independently need the same things: the model families done properly, the full battery of
validation tests with their formulas and references, the regulatory and accounting impact of each model, and a trace of the outcome. They also need to know
how far a passed or a failed test can be trusted, a question that the texts of the supervisors leave open. The reporting instructions of the European Central Bank fix the
statistics and the p-values of the back-testing of the probability of default and set no pass or fail threshold \citep{credit-scoring:ecb2019}, and the Basel
Committee working paper that introduced most of these tests concludes that no really powerful tests of adequate calibration are available
\citep{credit-scoring:bcbswp14}.

\paragraph{What Contraste does and what this report covers.} Contraste is a workbench that builds the main families of bank risk models, runs the validation
battery that a validator and a supervisor would run on each of them, and shows what each model changes in capital, provisions, liquidity or decisions. It
answers, for \rsCases{} authored cases, the question of what a model says, whether it passes the tests, what it changes and what its limits are. The cases span
\rsCategories{} categories, from credit scoring and rating transitions to provisions, loss given default and exposure at default, portfolio capital, market risk,
counterparty credit risk, interest-rate and liquidity risk of the banking book, operational risk, capital aggregation and stress testing, machine-learning and
fairness validation, and the validation of the validator itself. \rsCasesResult{} cases carry committed results; case \rsCaseNoResult{} is planned and has none. The report is the complete technical account:
it states each method with its theory and its parameters, each data contract, the pipeline, the protocol shared by all cases, the results of every case and their
limits.

\paragraph{Contributions.} The report makes the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item It implements, runs and compares the methods of \rsMethodRows{} entries of the method ladder on public or synthetic data of known truth, in sections~\ref{sec:credit-scoring}
to~\ref{sec:validation-tests}, each with its equations, its algorithm, its parameters and the criterion by which it is accepted.
\item It measures the size and the power of the validation tests on planted defects, so that a light of a test can be read with its error rates
(\cref{sec:bs1}), and reruns the published studies that measured them before (\cref{sec:m37}).
\item It reconciles the engine with reference engines and with the printed answers of the papers, and reports the gaps instead of hiding them (\cref{sec:bs2}).
\item It fixes a leakage-safe evaluation protocol for each case and states what each cannot show (\cref{sec:bs3}).
\item It adds engines and test constructions that no maintained open toolkit provides, among them the Chilean indexation unit in the interest-rate risk of the banking
book (\cref{sec:bs7}), a test of a behavioural assumption changed at a limit breach (\cref{sec:bs8}), the dependence band shown with every aggregate capital figure
(\cref{sec:bs9}) and a reverse liquidity stress with a refusal rule (\cref{sec:bs11}).
\item It proposes four methods and reports each whatever its result: a scale-and-level complement to the profit-and-loss attribution test (\cref{sec:bs13}), an
anchored tipping-point default probability under informative rating withdrawals (\cref{sec:bs14}), e-process sequential monitors for calibration and back-tests
(\cref{sec:bs15}) and adaptive conformal recalibration of the value at risk (\cref{sec:bs16}).
\item It collects the findings that the validation raised on the \rsCasesResult{} cases, \rsFindTotal{} in all, with their severity and the opinion level of each case
(\cref{sec:results}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Organisation.} Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the validation problem formally and the scope of the product, and Section~\ref{sec:notation} fixes the notation.
Section~\ref{sec:data} lists the data sources and the splits, and Section~\ref{sec:architecture} describes the system. The methods follow in
Sections~\ref{sec:credit-scoring} to~\ref{sec:proposed}, one section for each family of models and one for the product's own contributions, each ending with its results.
Section~\ref{sec:protocol} states the protocol that every case shares, Section~\ref{sec:results} collects the results across the cases, and
Section~\ref{sec:validation} explains how those results are known to be correct. Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:limitations} discuss the findings and their
limits, Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility} gives the commands that rebuild every table and figure, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} concludes. The data contracts are in
Appendix~\ref{sec:contracts}.
