\section{Methods: lifetime PD and expected credit loss}
\label{sec:provisions}

A bank covers the credit losses it expects with an allowance, a balance-sheet amount whose size moves the reported profit, the
capital and, through the staging rules, the timing of every loss. The amount has no market price. It is the output of a chain of
models: a default probability for each loan and each period of its remaining life, a loss severity, an exposure profile, a
discount rate, a set of macroeconomic scenarios with weights, and a rule that decides which loans carry a lifetime loss and which
a twelve-month loss. The supervisor therefore reads the allowance as a model-risk object, not as an accounting entry. The Basel
Committee asks that the models behind the allowance be validated at development, on significant change and at least yearly
\citep{provisions:bcbs2015}. The European Banking Authority found in its monitoring of 2021 and 2023 a wide diversity of practice in the
assessment of significant increases in credit risk, in the modelling of default probabilities and in forward-looking information, and
reported that a large share of institutions had backtested nothing, or only the twelve-month parameters
\citep{provisions:eba2021,provisions:eba2023}. The European Central Bank reported that roughly a quarter of the loss coverage of the
performing books it reviewed came from post-model overlays \citep{provisions:ecb2024}.

Two regimes govern the allowance in the cases of this chapter. IFRS 9 prescribes the measurement objective, a probability-weighted
and discounted amount that uses reasonable and supportable forward-looking information, and prescribes no formula
\citep{provisions:ifrs9,provisions:gppc2016}; it fixes two presumptions, a significant increase in credit risk after more than thirty
days past due and default after ninety days. The Chilean Comision para el Mercado Financiero applies IFRS 9 to the financial reporting of banks
with one exception: the impairment chapter is not applied to loans and contingent credits, whose provisions follow Chapter B-1 of the
Compendio de Normas Contables para Bancos, on statutory tables \citep{provisions:cmfcncb}. The Banco de Espana, in Annex 9 of
Circular 4/2017, applies the IFRS 9 expected-loss model and publishes, for entities without internal methodologies, coverage
percentages of the amount not covered by guarantees \citep{provisions:bde2017,provisions:bde2021}. A validator who meets a provision must first know which of these regimes produced it.

Three cases of Contraste exercise the methods of this chapter. Case C06 asks how lifetime default probability, staging and
expected credit loss behave across six reporting dates of a public portfolio, and what a validator finds when the model of each date
is tested on what happened after it; the portfolio is the loan file of the U.S. Small Business Administration 7(a) programme, a public
twin of a mortgage book, because the mortgage file of Freddie Mac could not be used. The loans are small-business loans, not
mortgages, and default is a charge-off. Case C07 asks what one portfolio costs under the Chilean statutory tables, under IFRS 9 and
under the Spanish coverage tables; the portfolio is a synthetic bank, Banco Andino, calibrated to aggregates of the CMF. Case C03 uses the same SBA file
to ask a narrower question, the default probability by approval vintage through 2008. It matters here because it shows, on the
same loans, how far the default rate of a vintage moves with the economy (Figure~\ref{fig:prov-vintages}), which is the movement that
the macroeconomic covariates of the lifetime models and the scenario weights of the expected credit loss are meant to carry.

\subsection{Lifetime default probability: Kaplan-Meier, discrete-time hazard and Cox rungs}
\label{sec:m10}

IFRS 9 needs the probability of default period by period over the remaining life of a loan, not a single annual figure. Let $\tau$ be the
default time, $S(t)=\Prob(\tau>t)$ the survival function with $S(0)=1$, and $h(t)$ the conditional probability of default in period $t$
given survival to the start of the period (the discrete hazard; the Global Public Policy Committee calls it the marginal probability of default, and Contraste
reserves ``marginal'' for the unconditional probability $q(t)$ below, always printing both names). The identities that connect them are
\begin{equation}
S(t)=\prod_{u=1}^{t}\bigl(1-h(u)\bigr),\qquad q(t)=S(t-1)\,h(t),\qquad F(t)=\sum_{u=1}^{t}q(u)=1-S(t),
\label{eq:prov-identities}
\end{equation}
with $F(t)$ the cumulative probability of default (\citealt{provisions:botha2025tutorial}, equations 7 to 10; \citealt{provisions:bellini2019}). Every rung
below produces a hazard or a cumulative curve, and the engine function \texttt{from\_hazard} of \texttt{riskvalidation} 1.1.0 turns a
hazard into the survival, marginal and cumulative curves that satisfy Equation~\ref{eq:prov-identities} to $10^{-12}$ \citep{provisions:botha2025tutorial}. The
survival analysis of credit data has a long literature: the Cox model for time to default \citep{provisions:cox1972,provisions:banasik1999,provisions:stepanova2002},
its extension with macroeconomic covariates \citep{provisions:bellotti2009}, the discrete-time hazard as a logit on loan-period data
\citep{provisions:allison1982,provisions:shumway2001,provisions:bellotti2013}, the benchmark of survival methods for time to default
\citep{provisions:dirick2017} and the mixture cure model for the borrowers that never default \citep{provisions:tong2012}. Contraste fits three of these
families on the same loans and keeps the neural rung of Section~\ref{sec:m33} beside them.

The first rung is the Kaplan-Meier product-limit estimate on loan age in months, one pooled curve for all loans selected at the cutoff,
$\hat S(t)=\prod_{t_j\le t}(1-d_j/n_j)$ with $d_j$ the defaults and $n_j$ the loans at risk at the event time $t_j$. The second rung is the
discrete-time hazard, a logit on loan-quarter cells aggregated by covariate stratum, age band and calendar quarter,
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{logit} h_i(a,c)=\alpha_{b(a)}+\bm\beta^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i+\bm\gamma^{\top}\mathbf{m}_{c-1},\qquad
\ell=\sum_{\text{cells}}\Bigl[e\log h+(n-e)\log(1-h)\Bigr],
\label{eq:prov-dth}
\end{equation}
where $a$ is the age of loan $i$ in quarters, $b(a)$ its age band, $c$ the calendar quarter, $\mathbf{x}_i$ the loan covariates, $\mathbf{m}_{c-1}$ the macroeconomic
variables of the previous quarter, and $e$ and $n$ the defaults and the exposure of a cell; the coefficients maximise the binomial log-likelihood $\ell$. This is the
only rung that conditions on a scenario, because the future macroeconomic path enters the linear predictor. The third rung is the Cox
proportional hazards model with loan age in months as the time scale and no macroeconomic input, because the statsmodels implementation has no time-varying
covariates. With the baseline cumulative hazard $H_0$ estimated with Breslow ties, the probability of default within $k$ more months of a loan alive at age $a$ is
\begin{equation}
\Prob\bigl(\tau\le a+k\mid\tau>a,\mathbf{x}\bigr)=1-\exp\Bigl(-\bigl[H_0(a+k)-H_0(a)\bigr]\,e^{\bm\beta^{\top}\mathbf{x}}\Bigr),
\label{eq:prov-cox}
\end{equation}
and the Kaplan-Meier rung gives $1-\hat S(a+k)/\hat S(a)$ in the same role. The observed side of every comparison is the Kaplan-Meier cumulative default of the accounts
followed from the reporting date, with prepayment and maturity as censoring, and the Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence, which treats prepayment as a competing exit
\citep{provisions:fine1999}; the two coincide only when prepayment is rare.

The assumptions are the standard ones of each family: independent loans, a hazard that depends on the covariates only through the stated terms, and, for the Cox rung,
proportional hazards. A further assumption is a stated limit of the case: every rung treats prepayment as censoring, so each estimates the cause-specific default hazard and the term
structure carries no prepayment attrition; the engine function \texttt{competing\_risks} exists but the case does not call it. The covariates are the loan size in four bands, the term in four bands, twelve sector groups
(eleven dummy columns) and a revolver flag, which give $18$ dummy columns and $384$ strata ($4\times4\times12\times2$, \url{panel.py} line 43). The discrete-time hazard uses nine age bands with edges at
$2$, $4$, $6$, $8$, $12$, $16$, $24$ and $40$ quarters of loan age, the unemployment rate, the real GDP growth and the prime rate with a lag of one quarter, standardised on the training
quarters and limited to the training range widened by $25\%$ of the range on each side, so that a hazard is never extrapolated beyond the support of its training quarters
(\url{ladder.py} lines 28 to 35). It has \pvDthCoef{} coefficients and the Cox model \pvCoxCoef{}. A rung with fewer than $200$ loans or $30$ defaults is reported as not evaluated and never fitted. The three
macroeconomic scenarios are the baseline and severely adverse paths of the Federal Reserve 2026 supervisory scenarios, transposed in shape to each reporting date by adding their
change from the jump-off quarter to the last value known at the date, and an adverse path that is the midpoint of the two; beyond $13$ quarters a path reverts linearly to the baseline over
$12$ quarters. The weights are \pvScenWeights{} for the three scenarios, policy parameters of the case and not the Federal Reserve's.

The cost of each fit is linear in the data it reads: the Kaplan-Meier estimate sorts the $n$ loans once, $O(n\log n)$; the discrete-time hazard is an iteratively reweighted least squares fit on $C$ cells with $p$ coefficients, $O(Cp^{2})$ per
iteration, where $C=\pvDthCells$ cells stand for the $\pvDthLoans$ loans kept at December 2010; the Cox fit costs $O(np^{2})$ per Newton iteration with the Breslow ties. The implementation is
the module \url{pipeline/cases/c06/ladder.py} of the data pipeline (functions \texttt{fit\_km}, \texttt{fit\_hazard}, \texttt{fit\_cox}, \texttt{build\_cells}) on \texttt{statsmodels} 0.15.0,
and the curves pass through \url{riskvalidation.provisions.term_structure} 1.1.0. The rungs are compared with each other and against the observed
cumulative default, with the acceptance criterion the specification states: the cumulative probability of default is monotone, the calibration at $12$, $24$ and $36$ months agrees with the Aalen-Johansen
incidence by risk decile, and the c-index and the integrated Brier score are reported for the rungs that rank loans. Table~\ref{tab:prov-rungs} gives the scores of the four rungs at the six dates and
Section~\ref{sec:results-provisions} reads them. The parameters are not tuned per date: the loan covariates, the age bands, the lag and the support margin are constants of the module.

\subsection{IFRS 9 expected credit loss engine}
\label{sec:m11}

For an exposure $i$ in macroeconomic scenario $s$, the expected credit loss over a horizon of $T$ months is the sum of the discounted marginal losses \citep{provisions:gppc2016,provisions:ifrs9},
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\ECL_{i,s}(T)&=\sum_{t=1}^{T} q^{D}_{i,s}(t)\,\LGD_{i,s}(t)\,\EAD_{i,s}(t)\,DF_i(t),\\
DF_i(t)&=(1+r_i)^{-\delta(t)/12},\quad \delta(t)=m_t-\tfrac12\Delta_t,
\end{split}
\label{eq:prov-ecl}
\end{equation}
where $q^{D}(t)$ is the default incidence of period $t$ (Equation~\ref{eq:prov-identities}), $\LGD(t)\in[0,1]$ the loss given default as a fraction of $\EAD(t)$, the exposure at default in money, $r_i$ the original effective interest rate per year,
$m_t$ the end month of period $t$ and $\Delta_t$ its length in months, so that $\delta(t)$ is the mid-period convention. The horizon is $12$ months for a loan in Stage 1 and the remaining
contractual life, capped at $120$ months, for a loan in Stage 2. Discounting is to the reporting date at the original rate, never to the default date (IFRS 9 B5.5.44). The mid-period convention is a model setting and not a requirement of the standard: the engine
reproduces the discount factors $\pvWxDfOne$ and $\pvWxDfTwo$ at a rate of $6\%$ for months $3$ and $6$, and a golden loss of $\pvWxGoldenEcl$ for two periods of default incidence $0.01$, a loss given default of $0.4$ and an exposure of $100{,}000$.

The standard requires more than one scenario when the loss is non-linear in the macroeconomic path, and the allowance is the probability-weighted loss,
\begin{equation}
\ECL_i=\sum_{s=1}^{N_s}w_s\,\ECL_{i,s},\quad w_s\ge0,\ \sum_s w_s=1,\ N_s\ge2,\qquad
NL=\frac{\ECL_{\mathrm{weighted}}}{\ECL(\bar m)}-1,
\label{eq:prov-weighted}
\end{equation}
where $\bar m=\sum_s w_s m_s$ is the weighted mean macroeconomic path and $NL$ the non-linearity index of the portfolio, the excess of the weighted loss over the loss at the mean path. With a convex loss in the path, Jensen's
inequality gives $\E[\ECL(M)]\ge\ECL(\E[M])$ and so $NL\ge0$. The one-factor conditional probability of default makes the effect concrete \citep{ratings:gordy2003,ratings:bcbs2005}. With asset correlation $\rho$ and a standard-normal systematic factor $z$,
$\PD^{\mathrm{PIT}}(z)=\Phi\bigl((\Phi^{-1}(\PD^{\mathrm{TTC}})-\sqrt{\rho}\,z)/\sqrt{1-\rho}\bigr)$ with $\Phi$ the standard normal distribution function. For a through-the-cycle probability of $2\%$, $\rho=\pvRhoUsed$ and
scenarios $z=+1,0,-2$ with weights $0.25,0.50,0.25$, the engine returns the conditional probabilities $\pvWxPitUp\%$, $\pvWxPitMid\%$ and $\pvWxPitDown\%$; the weighted probability is $\pvWxWeighted\%$ and the probability at the weighted mean factor
$\bar z=\pvWxZbar$ is $\pvWxAtMean\%$, a ratio of $\pvWxRatio$ and $NL=\pvWxIndex$ at a loss given default of $40\%$ (a weighted loss rate of $\pvWxLossWeighted\%$ against $\pvWxLossMean\%$ for the average scenario). The scenario at $z=0$ is the median, not the mean, probability,
$\pvWxPitMid\%$ against the $2\%$ unconditional mean, which is why a base scenario must not be read as the expectation. Gauss-Hermite integration of the same function with $64$ nodes returns \pvWxGh{}, equal to the through-the-cycle input to $10^{-9}$.

The stage of each loan comes from the quantitative test of the engine. With $\overline{PD}=1-(1-PD)^{1/H_{\mathrm{yrs}}}$ the annualised lifetime probability over a remaining life of $H_{\mathrm{yrs}}$ years, a loan is in Stage 2 when
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{1}\Bigl[\tfrac{\overline{PD}^{\mathrm{rep}}}{\overline{PD}^{\mathrm{orig}}}\ge m\bigl(\overline{PD}^{\mathrm{orig}}\bigr)\wedge \overline{PD}^{\mathrm{rep}}-\overline{PD}^{\mathrm{orig}}\ge\delta\Bigr]\vee
\mathbb{1}\Bigl[\tfrac{\overline{PD}^{\mathrm{rep}}}{\overline{PD}^{\mathrm{orig}}}\ge3\Bigr],
\label{eq:prov-sicr}
\end{equation}
where the superscripts name the current estimate and the estimate at origination for the same remaining window, $m(\cdot)$ is a multiplier schedule that does not rise with the origination probability and $\delta$ an absolute floor. The ratio form follows the Basel guidance on the relative and absolute change in default probability \citep{provisions:bcbs2015}, and the threefold backstop is a supervisory
expectation of the European Banking Authority, not part of IFRS 9 \citep{provisions:eba2023}. Stage 3 holds the credit-impaired loans, and the allowance sums the stages and keeps overlays as separate signed lines,
$\mathrm{LA}=\sum_{S1}\ECL^{12m}+\sum_{S2}\ECL^{\mathrm{life}}+\sum_{S3}\ECL^{D}+\sum_{\mathrm{POCI}}\Delta\ECL+\sum_k\mathrm{OVL}_k$. The roll-forwards of IFRS 7 close when the residual
$r=\mathrm{closing}-(\mathrm{opening}+\sum_j\mathrm{movement}_j)$ is at most $10^{-6}\max(1,G)$ in absolute value, with $G$ the sum of the closing gross carrying amounts.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{The expected credit loss of a portfolio at a reporting date (cases C06 and C07).}
\label{alg:prov-ecl}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require loans with hazards $h_{i,s}$ for each scenario $s$, loss given default, exposure profile, original rate, weights $w_s$, staging policy
\For{each scenario $s$}
  \State build the term structure of every loan from its hazard path (Equation~\ref{eq:prov-identities})
  \State compute $\ECL^{12m}_{i,s}$ and $\ECL^{\mathrm{life}}_{i,s}$ by Equation~\ref{eq:prov-ecl}
\EndFor
\State weight the current cumulative probability over the scenarios and compute the origination expectation with the macroeconomic inputs frozen at origination
\State annualise both over the remaining life and allocate the stages by Equation~\ref{eq:prov-sicr}
\State take $\ECL_i$ by Equation~\ref{eq:prov-weighted}, with $\ECL^{12m}$ in Stage 1 and $\ECL^{\mathrm{life}}$ in Stage 2, with staging frozen at the weighted conditions
\State compute $NL$ from the loss at the weighted mean path; add the overlays as separate lines; close the roll-forwards
\Ensure allowance by stage, overlays, $NL$, roll-forward residuals
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Every assumption below is a policy parameter or an assumption of the case and is labelled as such. Probability of default, loss given default, exposure and discount rate are supplied by the caller. The multiplier schedule is $4.0$ up to an origination probability of
$0.01$, $2.5$ up to $0.05$ and $1.5$ up to $0.5$ in case C06 (up to $0.99$ in case C07), the absolute floor is $0.005$ and the threefold ratio is $3.0$; the probation after a cure is $0$ months in C06, whose file has no cure dates and whose dates are a year apart, and $6$ months in C07. No loan in the SBA file has a
delinquency history, so the thirty-day backstop never fires in C06. The loss given default of C06 is a charge-off severity proxy by term band, an upper bound because the file has no recovery after charge-off, and the exposure is a level-payment annuity at the effective rate because the file has no balance. The
period is $3$ months and the asset correlation $\rho=\pvLossRho$, the value of the worked example. In C07 the three weighting scenarios are $z=+1,0,-2$ with weights $0.25,0.50,0.25$ around current conditions, each variant adds its own shift to the factor, and the shock of the first year decays with a half-life of $4$ quarters afterwards.

For $A$ loans, $S$ scenarios and $T$ periods the work of Algorithm~\ref{alg:prov-ecl} is $O(A\,S\,T)$ with $T\le40$ quarterly periods, processed in chunks of $40{,}000$ loans. The implementation calls \url{riskvalidation.provisions} 1.1.0 for every formula
(\texttt{term\_structure}, \texttt{staging}, \texttt{ecl}, \texttt{overlays}, \texttt{reconciliation}) and \url{riskvalidation.validation.ifrs9} for the battery of nine tests; the pipeline modules \url{pipeline/cases/c06/run.py} and \url{pipeline/cases/c07/ifrs9.py} assemble the inputs and
sum. The acceptance criterion of the specification is that the reconciliation identities close within $10^{-6}$, that the weighted loss is at least the loss at the average scenario in the convex regime, that the worked example above is reproduced, and that overlays are separate lines. Section~\ref{sec:results-provisions} reports the
battery on the six dates of C06 and on the scenarios of C07.

\subsection{Chilean CMF Chapter B-1 provisions}
\label{sec:m12}

In the statutory accounts of a Chilean bank the provision on loans and contingent credits is the expected loss of Chapter B-1, not the IFRS 9 impairment: Chapter A-2 N$^{\circ}$ 5 of the Compendio states that IFRS 9 chapter 5.5 is not applied to
those assets, for prudential reasons, and applies to other financial assets such as debt instruments at amortised cost \citep{provisions:cmfcncb}. The engine therefore computes B-1 only, in the module \url{riskvalidation.provisions.local_cl}, and no loan provision
is computed as IFRS 9 anywhere in that module. B-1 has two model families, individual assessment of debtors and group assessment; group assessment applies to residential mortgages, consumer, student and the commercial exposures that satisfy both conditions of Circular 2.313 (an aggregate
exposure to the counterparty below $20{,}000$ UF and each aggregate exposure at most $0.2\%$ of the group pool), and other commercial exposures are assessed individually. The provision factor of the standard methods is the product of a probability of default
and a loss given default, both read from tables of the regulator,
\begin{equation}
PE=PI\cdot PDI,\qquad \mathrm{Provision}_{\mathrm{debtor}}=(EAP-EA)\,PI\,PDI+EA\,PI_{\mathrm{aval}}\,PDI_{\mathrm{aval}},
\label{eq:prov-b1}
\end{equation}
where $PI$ is the probability of default and $PDI$ the loss given default of the debtor's category, $PE$ the provision factor, $EAP$ the exposure subject to provisions (loans and contingent credits less collateral at realisable value) and $EA$ the guaranteed part, which takes the guarantor's category and is never deducted from
the exposure. All parameters are decimals in the engine; the tables print per cent. Table~\ref{tab:prov-b1} gives the ten individual categories as the engine holds them, A1 to A6 for normal debtors and B1 to B4 for substandard debtors. For debtors in default, the expected loss rate is
\begin{equation}
TPE=\frac{E-R}{E},\qquad \mathrm{Provision}=E\cdot PP(TPE),
\label{eq:prov-b1-default}
\end{equation}
with $E$ the exposure, $R$ the amount recoverable from collateral and collection, and $PP$ a step function of $TPE$ with six categories: $2\%$ up to a $TPE$ of $3\%$, $10\%$ up to $20\%$, $25\%$ up to $30\%$, $40\%$ up to $50\%$, $65\%$ up to $80\%$ and $90\%$ above, upper bounds inclusive. The group portfolios take the larger of the standard and the internal method unless the internal method carries the CMF's
no-objection, $\mathrm{Prov}_{\mathrm{group}}=\max(\mathrm{Prov}^{\mathrm{ME}},\mathrm{Prov}^{\mathrm{MI}})$, and Banco Andino has no internal method, so the standard applies. Consumer loans follow Circular 2.346 of 2024-03-06 \citep{provisions:cmf2346}, with a probability that depends on the debtor's maximum days past due in the bank,
whether it holds a residential mortgage in the system and whether it had a debt $30$ days or more past due in the system in the previous three months, in $16$ cells.

\subsection{Banco de Espana Annex 9 coverage tables}
\label{sec:m13}

Spanish credit institutions apply the IFRS 9 expected-loss model, and Annex 9 of Circular 4/2017 gives, for entities without internal methodologies for collective estimates, alternative solutions: a coverage percentage of the uncovered amount by risk segment and category
\citep{provisions:bde2017}. The three performing and impaired categories correspond to the IFRS 9 stages, as Circular 6/2021 states for reporting: normal risk is Stage 1 with the twelve-month loss, normal risk under special watch is Stage 2 with the lifetime loss, and doubtful risk is Stage 3; written-off risk is derecognised
\citep{provisions:bde2021}. The coverage of an operation $j$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Cobertura}_j=c_{s,k}\cdot\max\Bigl(0,\ \EAD_j-\sum_{g}\mathrm{ValorAjustado}_g\Bigr),
\label{eq:prov-annex9}
\end{equation}
where $c_{s,k}$ is the coverage percentage for segment $s$ and category or arrears bucket $k$, $\EAD_j$ the exposure and $\mathrm{ValorAjustado}_g$ the adjusted value of the effective guarantee $g$. Point 144 gives $c$ for normal and special-watch risk and point 140 gives $c$ for doubtful risk by the age of the oldest
past-due amount in seven buckets from more than $90$ days up to $6$ months to more than $21$ months. The regulation counts months and the engine converts them once into days,
\begin{equation}
d_m=\operatorname{round}(30.4375\,m),\qquad m\in\{6,9,12,15,18,21\},
\label{eq:prov-annex9-days}
\end{equation}
which gives the bucket upper bounds $183$, $274$, $365$, $457$, $548$ and $639$ days, inclusive; $30.4375$ is the mean length of a month in days and the convention is Contraste's, which a bank's own day count overrides. An account at $90$ days or fewer is not doubtful by arrears and the function raises, and a doubtful account for a reason other than arrears
has no transcribed coverage, so the account is reported as not evaluated and never filled with a placeholder number.

Annex 9 is a supervisory alternative, not the only route: an entity may use internal methodologies for its collective estimates, subject to validation, or compare them against the Annex 9 coverage by segment (point 65, as restated by Circular 1/2025), so the tables below are the coverage a bank without such a model applies and the coverage against which one with such a model may compare. The engine holds every coefficient as a decimal of the uncovered amount, while the regulation and the tables of this report print per cent; no coefficient is computed, so the engine adds no estimation error and a wrong value could only come from a transcription error, which the cell-by-cell assertion against the fixture is there to catch.

Table~\ref{tab:prov-annex9} lists the $\pvAnnexSegments$ segments, from A.1.1 construction and real-estate development to B.3 other purposes, with the coverage the engine returns at the bucket reached at $120$, $220$, $300$, $400$, $500$, $600$ and $700$ days. The table shows that the percentages are steepest for development finance, $30\%$ for special watch against $1.9\%$ for normal
risk in segment A.1.1, and gentlest for housing, $18\%$ against $0.7\%$; it does not show the haircut table for effective guarantees, which the engine does not transcribe. \subsection{PC-Hazard neural discrete-time survival}
\label{sec:m33}

The PC-Hazard model of \citet{provisions:kvamme2021} assumes a hazard that is piecewise constant on a grid of intervals and lets a feed-forward network map the covariates of a loan to the hazard rate of every interval; related neural survival models are DeepSurv \citep{provisions:katzman2018} and the discrete-time models of
\citet{provisions:kvamme2019}. For a loan with covariates $\mathbf{x}$ the network returns rates $\lambda_j(\mathbf{x})>0$ per month for the intervals $j=1,\dots,J$, interval $j$ covering the ages $[w(j-1),wj)$ with width $w=3$ months, and the last interval open. The cumulative hazard is
the piecewise-linear function
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
H(t\mid\mathbf{x})&=\sum_{j<j(t)}w\,\lambda_j(\mathbf{x})+\lambda_{j(t)}(\mathbf{x})\bigl(t-w\,(j(t)-1)\bigr),\\
\Prob(\tau\le a+k\mid\tau>a,\mathbf{x})&=1-e^{-[H(a+k\mid\mathbf{x})-H(a\mid\mathbf{x})]},
\end{split}
\label{eq:prov-pch-hazard}
\end{equation}
with $j(t)$ the interval holding age $t$; the second expression is the lifetime probability that enters Equation~\ref{eq:prov-identities}, the counterpart of Equation~\ref{eq:prov-cox} without the proportionality. A loan observed for a time $t$, with default indicator $\epsilon$, has the log-likelihood
\begin{equation}
\ell(\theta)=\sum_{\text{loans}}\Bigl[\epsilon\,\log\lambda_{j(t)}(\mathbf{x})-\sum_{j}\lambda_j(\mathbf{x})\,\rho_j(t)\Bigr]=\sum_{s,j}\Bigl[d_{s,j}\log\lambda_j(\mathbf{x}_s)-\lambda_j(\mathbf{x}_s)\,\rho_{s,j}\Bigr],
\label{eq:prov-pch-likelihood}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_j(t)$ is the time the loan spent in interval $j$, $\theta$ the network weights, and in the second form $d_{s,j}$ and $\rho_{s,j}$ are the defaults and the months of exposure of stratum $s$ in interval $j$. This is the Poisson likelihood of the interval counts. The data therefore enter only through
two tables of the strata by intervals, the defaults and the exposure, so the training is exact, full batch and deterministic, and costs the same on $1.7$ million loans as on a few thousand. The loss minimised is the negative of Equation~\ref{eq:prov-pch-likelihood} per default.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{Training of the PC-Hazard network at a reporting date.}
\label{alg:prov-pch}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require loans selected at the cutoff with loan age $t$ and default flag $\epsilon$, stratum design matrix $X$, seed
\State draw a random fifth of the loans as hold-out; compute the default and exposure tables of Equation~\ref{eq:prov-pch-likelihood} for the training and the hold-out loans
\State initialise the last bias of the network at the inverse softplus of the pooled rate of each interval
\For{epoch $=1,\dots,1500$}
  \State $\lambda\leftarrow\operatorname{softplus}(\mathrm{net}(X))+10^{-10}$; evaluate the loss on the training tables; take one Adam step with weight decay $10^{-4}$
  \State evaluate the hold-out loss; if it improves, keep the weights and reset the counter; else increase the counter
  \If{the counter reaches $60$} \State \textbf{stop} \EndIf
\EndFor
\State restore the weights of the lowest hold-out loss; store the rates of every stratum and the SHA-256 of the weights
\Ensure rates $\lambda_j(\mathbf{x}_s)$ for all strata and intervals
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The assumptions are a hazard constant within each three-month interval, independent loans, and, as in the Cox rung, prepayment and maturity as censoring. The rung stands on the same information as the Cox rung, the loan covariates and the age in months, and trained on the loans known at the reporting date; what it adds is the shape, with no proportional hazards and a free age profile per stratum.
The network has $\pvNetInputs$ dummy-column inputs, two hidden layers of $\pvNetHidden$ ReLU units and $\pvNetOutputs$ softplus outputs, one positive rate per month for each of the $60$ intervals, which cover $15$ years, so it has $18\cdot32+32+32\cdot32+32+32\cdot60+60=\pvNetParams$ weights. The training uses Adam with a learning rate of $0.01$ and a weight decay of
$10^{-4}$, full batch in double precision on one CPU thread, at most $1500$ epochs and a patience of $60$ epochs on the hold-out loss of a seeded random fifth of the loans, and a rate floor of $10^{-10}$; the seed is $4506$ plus the day number of the reporting date. These hyper-parameters are constants of the pipeline module, identical at every date; the sources record no search over them, so how
they were chosen is not stated. One epoch evaluates the network on the $384$ strata rows and so costs about $384\times\pvNetParams$ multiply-adds, independent of the number of loans; building the two tables is $O(n)$ once. Table~\ref{tab:prov-pch-fit} gives the loans, the split, the epochs run and the final loss at each date.

\input{tables/provisions-t-pch-fit}

The implementation is \url{pipeline/cases/c06/neural.py} (functions \texttt{interval\_statistics}, \texttt{fit\_network}, \texttt{fit\_pch}, \texttt{conditional\_cumulative\_pd}) on \texttt{torch} 2.14.1 for the network, with the curves passed to \url{riskvalidation.provisions.term_structure} 1.1.0 and the weights identified by their hash. The comparison is with the Cox rung
on the same covariates, time scale and loans, at each of the six dates, on the accounts with at least $12$ quarters of contractual life left, so that the $36$-month curve of every account is observed in the prediction and, up to the follow-up, in the data. The acceptance criterion of the specification is the calibration at $12$, $24$ and $36$ months against the Aalen-Johansen incidence by risk decile, and the
c-index and the integrated Brier score beside Cox. The integrated Brier score is the mean over the observed horizons $k\in\{6,12,\dots,36\}$ of $\hat F(k)\,(1-\bar F(k))^{2}+(1-\hat F(k))\,\bar F(k)^{2}$, with $\bar F$ the predicted and $\hat F$ the observed cumulative default, computed from aggregates. A prediction was stated before the computation: the rung has a lower integrated Brier score and a smaller mean absolute decile
gap against Aalen-Johansen, averaged over $12$, $24$ and $36$ months, at more than half of the dates where both are observed, and it is refuted when this holds at half of them or fewer; the c-index is reported beside, outside the criterion. The result is in Section~\ref{sec:results-provisions}.
